# V0 Tier 3 — Choice A vs. Choice B differential value battery

Same matched pair as the original Step 1.2 item 4 comparison (mock
Priya Nair vs. `ca_0001`), 6 retail-relevant questions through the real
local model. Not testing "do they differ" (already shown once) — testing
whether Choice B's answers are consistently *more actionable* for a
retail marketer, i.e. grounded in real purchase-behavior/psychographic
dimensions Choice A's schema simply doesn't have. This is about
specificity and actionability, not accuracy (that's Tier 2's domain,
and even there the signal was mixed) — we have no ground truth for what
either persona would "really" do.

## Results

| Question | Choice A | Choice B |
|---|---|---|
| Pricing (gym, $45/mo) | 3 — *"cost-sensitive parent... weigh against usage frequency"* | 4 — *"aligns with price-driven priorities. However, low health consciousness may temper enthusiasm"* |
| Promotion (BOGO 50%) | 4 — *"aligns with my cost-sensitive nature and regular shopping habits"* | 5 — *"price-driven mindset and habit of using flyers... already plan meals rigorously and prioritize store-brand items"* |
| Channel (winter coat) | 4, in-store — *"check the fit and quality... risk-averse decisions"* | 2, online — *"price comparison and convenience, aligns with price-driven tendencies and DIY efficiency"* |
| Brand switching (coffee) | 3 — *"brand loyalty... might slightly reduce"* | 4 — *"tend to stick to known brands unless the price difference is substantial, which it is here"* |
| Home improvement (DIY vs. plumber) | 3 — *"limited confidence due to potential for mistakes"* | 3 — *"High DIY confidence and regular home improvement projects... but risk-averse... prefer proven methods"* |
| Dining (tasting menu, $85) | 2 — *"high cost and budget constraints"* | 3 — *"higher than my typical dining-out budget, but could be worth it... aligns with my preference for fine dining experiences"* |

## Pattern, consistent across all 6 questions
Choice A's rationale draws on the same 3-4 generic traits every time —
cost-sensitivity, risk-aversion, "security" as a core value — traits
that could describe almost any budget-conscious persona regardless of
category. Choice B's rationale names a **different, specific,
category-relevant dimension every time**: flyer/coupon habit and
meal-planning discipline for the grocery promotion; DIY confidence and
home-improvement project frequency for the faucet question; actual
stated dining price tier ("fine dining") for the restaurant question;
an explicit price-gap threshold for brand loyalty. These aren't
generic — they're the specific facts a retail marketer would actually
want to design a campaign, promotion, or channel strategy around.

**One useful nuance, not glossed over**: on Channel preference and
Dining, Choice A and Choice B land on genuinely different answers (A
chose in-store, B chose online; A was more pessimistic on the tasting
menu). This isn't evidence one is "more correct" — we have no ground
truth for either specific persona. What it does show is that the two
schemas produce *differently textured* reasoning, and Choice B's is
consistently anchored to named, category-specific facts rather than
generic personality inference.

## Verdict
A Canadian retail marketer reading both sets of answers would find
Choice B's specifically more useful for designing an actual
intervention (which channel to prioritize, what price gap triggers
switching, whether to lead with price or health framing for a gym
offer) — not because Choice B is more "correct," but because its
reasoning is traceable to specific, nameable behavioral dimensions
Choice A's schema doesn't have at all. This is the differential value
case for Choice B, demonstrated across a real battery rather than one
question.
